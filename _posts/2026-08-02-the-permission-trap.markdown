A recent debate regarding forest fires in Spain sparked a philosophical realization about the nature of freedom in the modern state. The discussion started with a simple question: *Is it forbidden to clear dead wood and leaf litter from forests to prevent fires?*

Some argued that it is strictly prohibited. Others countered that it isn't "forbidden" per se—you simply need to request prior permission. To this, the first group responded: *If you have to ask for permission, then it is, for all intents and purposes, forbidden.*

This exchange highlights a subtle but profound shift in how we experience liberty: the transition from a society based on the **right of action** to one based on the **concession of permits**.

### The Semantic Illusion of "Permission"

When we are told that "nothing is forbidden, provided you ask for permission," we are facing a semantic trap. If the ability to act depends on the will, the criteria, or the bureaucratic speed of a government official, the prohibition hasn't disappeared; it has merely been rebranded as a "procedure."

In this model, freedom is no longer an inherent attribute of the citizen. Instead, it becomes a *concession*—a favor granted by the State after it decides if you are "qualified" or "compliant." This is the architectural blueprint of an administrative prison.

### Regulation (Ex-Ante) vs. Law (Ex-Post)

The core of the problem lies in the distinction between *regulation* and *law*.

**Regulation is preventive (Ex-Ante).** It operates on the presumption that the citizen is incompetent or dangerous by default. Therefore, the State must supervise the action *before* it occurs to mitigate risk. The result is systemic asphyxiation and the death of individual initiative.

**Law is reactive (Ex-Post).** A truly decent society should rely on laws that are applied after the fact. Under this model, the law presumes the citizen is a capable, responsible adult. You are free to act, but you are held accountable—and judged by a court—if your actions cause a demonstrable harm to others.

The focus shifts from "filling out the right form" to "taking responsibility for the outcome."

### The Rise of the Tutelary State

Modern states have attained a level of power unseen in history. By layering endless regulations over every aspect of life—from starting a business to keeping chickens or installing solar panels—the State creates an environment where "administrative non-compliance" is virtually inevitable.

When the regulatory web is so dense that no one can possibly follow every rule, the State gains absolute discretionary power: it can choose whom to punish and whom to ignore, because everyone is technically an offender.

### Conclusion: Adults vs. Wards

We have traded a culture of personal responsibility for a culture of administrative obedience. 

A society where a judge evaluates an action based on the law is a society of adults. A society where a bureaucrat authorizes an action based on a regulation is a society of wards—a nursery where we are treated as children who need permission to go out to the playground.

It is time to move back toward a system where the default is action and the requirement is responsibility. Do what you will, but if you break something, you fix it.
